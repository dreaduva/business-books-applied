# Startup Experiment Playbook

[Home](../README.md) · [Book guide](../books/the-lean-startup/README.md) · [Worksheet](../books/the-lean-startup/worksheet.md)

Use this workflow to decide what to test, repair a weak plan, or interpret an experiment that has already run. The output is a traceable decision, with the original plan preserved alongside the results.

## 1. Name the investment decision

Write what you are considering committing: build time, a pilot, a new customer segment, or another bounded change. Identify the assumption that could make that investment a mistake. Separate known evidence from interpretation and record contrary information.

If the problem is still vague, use the [customer interview playbook](customer-interviews.md) first. If the task is a known implementation defect, fix it directly rather than forcing it into an experiment framework.

**Leave with:** a specific assumption and the decision it informs, plus related questions this test will not answer.

## 2. Compare ways to create the observation

Consider an offer page, prototype, manual service, narrow feature, or another intervention suited to the question. Compare their information value and burden. Do not choose the cheapest artifact if it cannot produce the needed evidence.

Describe what participants actually receive. If work is manual, make that clear. List prerequisites and quality constraints. A pilot that cannot be delivered as described needs repair before launch.

**Leave with:** one selected intervention, a rejected alternative, and the reason for the choice.

## 3. Define the measurement before the test

Specify the unit, eligibility, action counted, denominator, observation window, and source. Distinguish exposure, action, and later repeat opportunities. Include the operational burden that could make the intervention impractical.

Establish any baseline honestly. If it does not exist, record that the first test may establish it. Write how missing, duplicate, late, and excluded events will be handled.

**Leave with:** measurement definitions another person can apply without guessing.

## 4. Set a bounded decision rule

Explain why the rule fits the operating promise, economics, or uncertainty at stake. Name the cap, review owner, and stop conditions. Where there is no defensible threshold, propose a calibration exercise rather than inventing market validation criteria.

Do not run a real experiment merely because this plan is written. Resolve the relevant operational authorization and participant expectations first.

**Leave with:** an explicit plan for interpreting favorable, unfavorable, and inconclusive results.

## 5. Preserve the actual event record

During an authorized trial, record eligible events, outcomes, burden, and exceptions. Keep original identifiers. Do not replace inconvenient observations with cleaned-up summaries. Mark changes in the intervention version.

**Leave with:** a result log that distinguishes observation from inference and keeps missingness visible.

## 6. Review outcomes and explanations

Calculate raw fractions and relevant effort measures. Assess each original rule separately. Examine recruitment, execution, measurement, and hypothesis explanations. A high outcome rate with unaffordable delivery work is a mixed result, not automatic success.

Use the [worked example](../books/the-lean-startup/worked-example.md) to see an acknowledgment rate of 10/12 fail its local operational gates. Those numbers are fictional; the useful part is how the reasoning preserves both outcomes and burden.

**Leave with:** the narrow supported conclusion and the strongest contrary evidence.

## 7. Choose the next commitment

Decide whether to repair the test, continue a bounded investment, change a substantive assumption, stop, or obtain a particular missing fact. Record what changes in the next test and what stays fixed. Explain any revised rule rather than retroactively moving the target.

**Leave with:** a next action with a reason, owner, cap, and an observation that could change the decision.
