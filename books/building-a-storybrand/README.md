A visitor should be able to tell who a page is for, what it helps them do, and what happens after clicking its main button. This toolkit turns a messaging discussion into a **landing-page draft you can review line by line**.

## Summary

Donald Miller's StoryBrand framework puts the customer at the center of the story and the business in a supporting role. Its seven elements connect a customer's goal and problem with a guide, a plan, a call to action, the consequences of leaving the problem unresolved, and a successful outcome. The practical lesson is clarity: describe the customer's situation and a comprehensible way forward instead of making the visitor decipher a company biography.

## From framework to page

The worksheet below is our original writing tool, not a reproduction of the official BrandScript. It asks for five concrete outputs: audience and problem, supportable credibility, a path to use the product, publishable copy, and a review plan.

Start with one visitor and one primary action. A page asking someone to book a demonstration needs to explain the meeting; a self-service signup page needs to explain access, payment, and setup. “Get started” can conceal these differences. Use a button label that tells the visitor what actually happens.

| Page element | Question to answer | Example for a confirmation tracker |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headline | What can I do here? | See which lesson changes still need a reply |
| Supporting line | Is this relevant to my situation? | A pending-confirmation view for independent tutors |
| Demonstration | Why believe the description? | A labeled screenshot of the real product |
| Steps | What effort is required? | Add a change, record a reply, review pending items |
| Primary action | What happens next? | Request a walkthrough |

## Make the claims smaller and stronger

If the product displays unresolved confirmations, say that. “Never lose a customer again” is broader than the product can support. Testimonials must come from actual customers with permission to use them; a new product can show a functioning demonstration instead. Do not turn a fictional example from this repository into social proof.

Describe the cost of an unresolved problem proportionately. A missed reply is an operational inconvenience; it does not justify telling visitors their business will collapse. Clarity works better when the reader can judge the consequence for themselves.

## A useful review before publishing

Read only the headline, supporting line, and button. Can a new reader explain the offer without guessing? Then check the setup steps against the real product. Finally, highlight every factual claim and attach its evidence or remove it. Test comprehension separately from conversion: someone understanding the page does not establish that they will buy.

## Work through the message before choosing the layout

The seven-part sequence is a useful diagnostic: customer, problem, guide, plan, action, failure, success. The original book's [publisher sample](https://cdn.cokesbury.com/samples/pdf/9780718033323.pdf) identifies these elements and explains the emphasis on a clear, customer-relevant message. The workflow below is our own application to an early product, rather than an official BrandScript or an account of every chapter.

### Choose a customer and an immediate job

Write the situation that brings someone to the page. “Independent tutors checking rescheduled lessons” is useful because it points to an observable task. “Busy professionals seeking freedom” leaves the writer guessing about the product, evidence and next step. If you serve several audiences, choose the audience for this page; do not force all their different problems into one sentence.

Keep the desired outcome close enough to your product to explain the connection. A confirmation list can make outstanding replies visible. It cannot, by itself, promise a thriving tutoring business. That distinction makes the headline more specific and the proof easier to supply.

Before writing, record three inputs: who visits, what they currently do, and what action this page should enable. If any is unknown, the result is a provisional message to investigate, not publication-ready copy. Use the [positioning toolkit](../obviously-awesome/README.md) when the unresolved question is which customer values the product against which alternative.

### Give the problem a factual foundation

Describe an external difficulty first: several lesson changes are scattered across message threads. You can explore whether that produces uncertainty or frustration, but do not present a writer's guess about feelings as customer research. A quotation must come from a real supplied source, and permission to use it publicly is a separate matter.

A broader principle can give the message coherence—such as wanting agreements to be easy to check—but it should not become a moral judgment on the visitor. The practical cost of uncertainty might be another round of checking. There is no need to imply that a tutor who misses a reply is careless or unprofessional.

Write a problem paragraph that a customer could disagree with. “You reopen threads to see which changes still need a reply” is testable. “Your workflow is broken” mainly expresses the company's opinion. If target readers say the described task is rare, investigate relevance before polishing the sentence.

### Make the business a credible helper

Show understanding through accurate details of the work. Then show what the product can actually do. For an early prototype, a demonstration of adding a change and marking it confirmed is legitimate capability evidence. An impressive founder biography may be less useful to this particular decision.

Match evidence to the exact claim. A screenshot supports the existence of a screen; it does not establish that customers save an hour. A testimonial about friendly support does not establish a no-show reduction. If there is no proof for a numerical outcome, remove the number or describe the capability that is demonstrable.

This is also where boundaries belong. If the tutor must update replies manually, place that fact near the product explanation, not in an obscure footnote. Clear boundaries may reduce unsuitable requests while improving the quality of the remaining conversations.

### Distinguish access steps from usage steps

A visitor may need to understand two different plans. Access could involve requesting a demonstration, receiving a reply and attending a call. Usage could involve adding a changed lesson, recording its reply and checking outstanding items. Blending them makes a request form sound like instant product access.

Use the access plan near the primary button. Put the usage plan beside the demonstration. Keep steps concrete enough to check against actual operations. “Transform your workflow” is an aspiration; “add the new lesson time” is an action.

Select the primary action according to readiness. A prototype without self-service accounts should not use “Start free” unless immediate free access is genuinely provided. “Request a walkthrough” is more accurate. A secondary preview can help a cautious visitor understand the offer, but it should support rather than compete with the main decision.

### Show a proportionate before and after

Name the ordinary consequence of doing nothing and the observable improvement the product supports. For LessonLoop, outstanding replies remain scattered unless someone gathers them; a maintained list makes those outstanding items visible. Whether that improvement is worth another manual tool remains a question for the customer.

The page does not need seven framework headings. A short page can carry these ideas in a headline, explanation, demonstration, access steps and button. Use the framework to find missing meaning, then choose a layout that suits the visitor's decision. Avoid repeating the same benefit in seven different forms merely to fill the structure.

## Turn the brief into finished copy

Draft the headline and supporting line together. A concise headline often needs the supporting line to establish audience and mechanism. Read them with the button: the three pieces should describe one offer and one next step.

Then write the whole page, including labels that are easy to neglect: screenshot caption, form introduction, button and confirmation message. The experience after clicking must fulfill the promise before clicking. If the form is a request rather than a confirmed appointment, say so on both sides.

Keep a separate claim ledger while editing. For each factual sentence, record its basis, whether it is verified for this product, and whether the evidence supports the same scope. Unknown claims remain blocked or are rewritten; they do not become true because they sound plausible. This ledger belongs in the working brief, not necessarily on the public landing page.

A voice pass comes after the factual pass. Replace vague adjectives with concrete actions, read sentences aloud, and remove repeated assurances. Preserve the actual product vocabulary when it helps recognition. “Polished” is not a reason to replace an ordinary customer phrase with marketing jargon.

## Review comprehension without mistaking it for demand

Show the draft without first explaining the product. Ask the reader what it does, what work they would still do, and what happens after the button. Record their answer before correcting it. A leading question such as “Is it clear this is manual?” tells them the answer you were trying to test.

Classify feedback before revising. A wrong expectation about automation calls for a clearer mechanism. An accurate description followed by “I don't need this” points toward relevance or positioning. Trouble submitting the form is a flow problem. Each requires a different change.

Use small qualitative checks to discover misunderstandings, not to announce a statistically reliable improvement. If the page later goes live, define relevant visitors, the intended action and how you will identify qualified requests. A comprehension exercise alone supplies no conversion rate.

| Symptom | First thing to inspect | Useful correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Readers cannot name the product's job | Headline and supporting line | Name the concrete task and audience |
| Readers expect an unavailable feature | Mechanism and demonstration | State what happens manually beside the claim |
| Readers understand but do not care | Audience and actual problem | Revisit relevance; do not just intensify the promise |
| Many clicks, few appropriate requests | Button promise and form | Explain the actual commitment and eligibility |
| Page sounds like every competitor | Specific facts in the brief | Restore the distinctive workflow or supported capability |
| Every section repeats the same idea | Page outline | Give each section a different question to answer |

## Frequently asked questions

**Is this a StoryBrand template?** It is an independent worksheet for applying customer-centered messaging. The official framework and workbook are linked below.

**Does every page need all seven elements as headings?** No. Use them to examine the message; choose a page structure that suits the decision and reading context.

**Which edition is pictured?** The original 2017 edition. This toolkit does not claim to summarize additions in Building a StoryBrand 2.0.
