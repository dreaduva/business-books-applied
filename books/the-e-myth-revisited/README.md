If a recurring task only works when the founder remembers every detail, hiring someone does not automatically solve the problem. This toolkit helps you document **one process another person can follow and challenge**.

## Summary

Michael E. Gerber distinguishes doing skilled work from building a business that delivers that work consistently. The entrepreneur considers direction, the manager organizes execution, and the technician performs the work. The EMyth approach emphasizes developing systems so the business depends less on the owner's constant intervention. The practical starting point here is small: choose a recurring task, make the desired result explicit, and improve how it is performed.

## Work on one process that already exists

Choose a task with a recognizable trigger and output: onboarding a customer, preparing a proposal, or reviewing a support request. Avoid documenting the whole business at once. A short process with an owner and a usable exception path is more valuable than a polished manual nobody can operate.

Observe the task before writing instructions. Note the decisions the experienced person makes without mentioning them. “Set up the customer” hides questions about missing information, access, data handling, and what counts as finished.

| Process element | What to record | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trigger | The event that starts work | Prevents guessing when to act |
| Inputs | Information and access needed | Exposes blockers early |
| Steps | Observable actions in order | Makes the task repeatable |
| Acceptance | Evidence that the result is usable | Prevents premature completion |
| Exceptions | Stop, ask, or escalate conditions | Preserves judgment when the usual path fails |

## Roles do not require three hires

A solo founder may perform all three roles. The distinction helps identify which kind of work is missing. Completing every onboarding call is execution. Reviewing recurring setup failures is management. Deciding which customers the business should serve is strategic work. Document the responsibility, not an imaginary organizational chart.

## Test a handoff, not just a document

Give the draft to someone with the expected skill and access. Observe where they pause, infer missing steps, or need the founder. Those moments reveal defects in the process. A simulated walkthrough can uncover ambiguity, but a live process still needs an authorized trial and review.

Standardization should support a consistent result while leaving room for relevant judgment. Add an exception path for cases the procedure cannot safely resolve. Do not automate a step merely because it is repetitive; first understand the errors and permissions involved.

## Technician, manager and entrepreneur in the same week

EMyth's [three-role explanation](https://www.emyth.com/inside/the-three-business-personalities-entrepreneur-manager-technician) distinguishes delivery work, organizing people and systems, and setting business direction. Its [discussion of the book's core ideas](https://www.emyth.com/inside/groundbreaking-ideas-from-the-e-myth-revisited) challenges the assumption that technical competence alone prepares someone to run the business around that work. This toolkit applies those ideas to one operational handoff; the procedure format below is our own.

Consider onboarding a customer. Entering their information is technician work. Defining the required information, assigning ownership and inspecting recurring errors is management work. Deciding whether this customer segment and service model belong in the business is entrepreneurial work. A solo founder can do all three, but doing more data entry does not automatically resolve an unclear service model.

Look for the role that a recurring failure needs. A missing setup field may need a better intake rule. A customer asking for something outside the offer may need a scope decision. Neither is fixed by telling the operator to be more careful. The role distinction is useful when it changes the next action; it is not a personality test or a demand to hire three people.

## Select a process with an observable result

Start where repeated uncertainty creates delay, rework or inconsistent customer experience. A process should have a recognizable trigger and finish. “Improve operations” has neither. “Prepare a confirmed customer setup from an approved request” has both, provided approved and prepared are defined.

Choose work you can inspect. If the business has never delivered this service, describe a proposed process and its assumptions. If the founder currently performs it, watch or reconstruct a recent case using actual records the user supplies. Capture the questions the founder answers from memory: which version of a form is current, who can approve an exception, and what proves the customer received the right output.

Keep the boundary small enough to test. Onboarding need not include acquisition, invoicing, product development and every support incident. Define the interfaces with those activities: what is received, from whom, in what state, and what is handed back. This creates a useful seam for delegation.

## Write decisions as well as actions

An instruction such as “check the details” is incomplete until it says which details, against what source and what happens if they disagree. Write an observable action and its outcome: compare the requested time and time zone with the confirmed appointment record; if either is missing, mark the task blocked and ask the owner to resolve it.

Use explicit prerequisites rather than expecting the operator to improvise missing information. An empty field is not permission to guess. At the same time, do not make every harmless choice require founder approval. Specify what the operator can decide within scope and what requires an exception decision.

A compact decision table can be clearer than a long paragraph. Separate normal steps from uncommon branches, and keep the most consequential stop conditions visible near the relevant step. The goal is that an appropriately trained operator can perform the job, not that any person can perform specialized work without training.

## Define quality before speed

State what the customer or next operator should receive and how to verify it. Sending an onboarding email may be an activity; confirmed access to the correct resource is a result. “Done” should not depend only on the sender pressing a button.

Choose a small set of acceptance checks tied to the output. For a setup handoff, these might be the correct customer label, agreed time zone, usable access and a recorded next action. Do not add checks merely because a checklist looks more thorough when it is long.

Timing is useful when its meaning is clear. Record active work separately from waiting for missing information. A process that appears faster because it bypasses the missing-input check may generate more rework. Inspect output quality and exceptions alongside time before celebrating a speed improvement.

## Make ownership survive a handoff

Name the role accountable for completion and the person or role performing each part. These can be the same in a small business. Where work passes to another role, specify what is handed over, how it is acknowledged and who owns it until acknowledgement.

A procedure that says “notify support” can leave a task orphaned. A better handoff identifies the ticket or record, required context, expected acknowledgement and fallback if no one accepts it. Use the user's actual roles; if the business has no support team, do not invent one to make the diagram look complete.

Authority matters as much as access. An operator might be able to edit a record but lack authority to change the customer's purchased scope. Document the decision owner, not just the software button. The process can continue on unaffected work while a material decision remains blocked, if that split is explicit.

## Test the procedure with a difficult ordinary case

Choose a normal case and a plausible exception. Give the operator only the documented inputs, relevant training and access. Observe where the written process requires an inference. Do not rescue the walkthrough immediately; record the missing rule so the procedure can improve.

A desk simulation is valuable for finding ambiguity, but it does not prove real operational reliability. Label it accordingly. A live trial requires the appropriate authorization, realistic inputs and observation of the actual output. Save the evidence at its true scope.

Revise the smallest missing decision. If the failure involved a missing time zone, add an intake requirement and blocked-state path. Do not respond by adding a dozen unrelated approvals. Repeat the affected case after changing it and retain the version history so operators can tell which instructions are current.

## Keep the system useful after its first version

Assign a document owner and a review trigger: repeated exception, changed product behavior, new customer commitment or a failed handoff. Record the current version in one maintained location. Copies without an update path quietly become different processes.

When an operator finds a better method, capture what changed and whether the same quality standard still holds. Standardization should make improvement easier to evaluate. It should not require preserving a bad step because it was written down first.

| Recurring symptom | Diagnosis to investigate | Process change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Founder answers the same question repeatedly | Hidden decision rule | Document the rule and an example |
| Tasks disappear between roles | Ownership gap | Require handoff acceptance and fallback |
| Work is marked done but unusable | Activity mistaken for acceptance | Define and verify the output |
| Every unusual request becomes an emergency | No exception route | Add a blocked state and named decision owner |
| Fast completion creates rework | Speed displaced quality | Review first-pass acceptance and defects |
| Nobody follows the long manual | Scope or usability problem | Keep a concise operating path with focused references |

The finished artifact is one usable procedure with a boundary, inputs, ownership, steps, acceptance and exceptions. A larger manual can grow from validated processes later; it should not precede the ability to hand off one real task.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a checklist enough?** Sometimes. Use the smallest document that includes the trigger, actions, owner, quality check, and exception path. Complex work may also need examples and training.

**Should every task be standardized?** Start with recurring work where inconsistency causes problems. Exploration and unusual customer situations may need different treatment.

**When is a process finished?** A first version is ready for use when someone can execute it under its stated conditions. Keep ownership and a review trigger because conditions change.
