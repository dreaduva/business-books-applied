If a recurring task only works when the founder remembers every detail, hiring someone does not automatically solve the problem. This toolkit helps you document **one process another person can follow and challenge**.

## Summary

Michael E. Gerber distinguishes doing skilled work from building a business that delivers that work consistently. The entrepreneur considers direction, the manager organizes execution, and the technician performs the work. The EMyth approach emphasizes developing systems so the business depends less on the owner's constant intervention. The practical starting point here is small: choose a recurring task, make the desired result explicit, and improve how it is performed.

## Work on one process that already exists

Choose a task with a recognizable trigger and output: onboarding a customer, preparing a proposal, or reviewing a support request. Avoid documenting the whole business at once. A short process with an owner and a usable exception path is more valuable than a polished manual nobody can operate.

Observe the task before writing instructions. Note the decisions the experienced person makes without mentioning them. “Set up the customer” hides questions about missing information, access, data handling, and what counts as finished.

| Process element | What to record | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trigger | The event that starts work | Prevents guessing when to act |
| Inputs | Information and access needed | Exposes blockers early |
| Steps | Observable actions in order | Makes the task repeatable |
| Acceptance | Evidence that the result is usable | Prevents premature completion |
| Exceptions | Stop, ask, or escalate conditions | Preserves judgment when the usual path fails |

## Roles do not require three hires

A solo founder may perform all three roles. The distinction helps identify which kind of work is missing. Completing every onboarding call is execution. Reviewing recurring setup failures is management. Deciding which customers the business should serve is strategic work. Document the responsibility, not an imaginary organizational chart.

## Test a handoff, not just a document

Give the draft to someone with the expected skill and access. Observe where they pause, infer missing steps, or need the founder. Those moments reveal defects in the process. A simulated walkthrough can uncover ambiguity, but a live process still needs an authorized trial and review.

Standardization should support a consistent result while leaving room for relevant judgment. Add an exception path for cases the procedure cannot safely resolve. Do not automate a step merely because it is repetitive; first understand the errors and permissions involved.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a checklist enough?** Sometimes. Use the smallest document that includes the trigger, actions, owner, quality check, and exception path. Complex work may also need examples and training.

**Should every task be standardized?** Start with recurring work where inconsistency causes problems. Exploration and unusual customer situations may need different treatment.

**When is a process finished?** A first version is ready for use when someone can execute it under its stated conditions. Keep ownership and a review trigger because conditions change.
