A buyer needs to understand what they receive, why it could help, and what they must contribute. The business needs to know whether it can deliver. This toolkit produces an **offer brief with scope, proof, price assumptions, and capacity checks**.

## Summary

Alex Hormozi's value equation connects the desired outcome and perceived likelihood of achieving it with the time and effort involved. The offer-design implication is to improve the substance and credibility of the result while reducing unnecessary delay and customer work. It is a thinking framework for perceived value, not a calculator that predicts revenue or establishes a correct price.

## Use the value equation without inventing a score

Review four questions: What outcome does this buyer want? What makes the proposed path credible? When can the buyer reasonably experience progress? What work must they do? Write concrete answers instead of assigning arbitrary numbers and calling the result a value score.

For a small software pilot, reducing effort might mean helping the customer configure their first workflow. That assistance can also increase delivery cost. The worksheet keeps those two sides together so an attractive offer does not become an impossible service promise.

| Offer choice | Customer advantage | Delivery question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assisted setup | Less initial configuration | Who provides it and for how long? |
| A narrow pilot | A manageable first decision | What does success actually mean? |
| A product demonstration | Visible evidence of capability | Does it reflect the current product? |
| Clear exclusions | Fewer surprises | Can sales and delivery enforce the scope? |

## Specify the offer before choosing the headline

List deliverables, timing, customer responsibilities, exclusions, price, and payment terms. Separate a product's capabilities from the business outcome the buyer hopes to achieve. “A configured confirmation tracker” is deliverable; “no missed lessons” depends on behavior beyond the product.

A proposed guarantee also needs an operational check: what event triggers it, who decides, and can the business honor it? The worksheet can flag unanswered questions, but it does not draft legal terms. Do not invent a deadline or limited quantity merely to make the offer more urgent.

## Test whether the offer is workable

Estimate delivery time with stated assumptions. Add a reasonable allowance for support and inspect the worst plausible workload. Compare it with available capacity before opening more slots. A price minus direct costs is only a contribution estimate; it is not net profit and does not settle willingness to pay.

The completed example intentionally keeps its first offer narrow. It does not pile on unrelated bonuses. Each included activity helps the customer start using the product and has a defined limit.

## Four levers, four different design questions

The author's [value checklist](https://www.acquisition.com/hubfs/Offer%20Checklists%20-%20PDF%20Downloads/Pricing-Value-Checklist.pdf?hsLang=en) emphasizes the desired result, confidence in reaching it, time and effort. Our application treats each as a question about a real buyer and a real delivery process. None supplies a conversion forecast.

### Start with progress the buyer recognizes

Describe the buyer's situation before listing your services. A tutor may want to stop searching through several message threads before tomorrow's lessons. “A dashboard” is the deliverable; knowing what still needs a reply is the intended progress. The difference helps you decide whether setup assistance, a checklist or a new feature addresses the actual obstacle.

Choose a sufficiently narrow outcome for the first offer. “A configured list of outstanding lesson changes” can be checked at handoff. “A stress-free business” has no clear acceptance point. The narrower result is not automatically more valuable, but it can be explained, delivered and tested without guessing about everything else in the customer's life.

Record the current alternative. If a spreadsheet already solves the problem adequately, adding ten bonuses may not create a compelling reason to switch. You may need a different buyer, a more relevant service or a better product. Pricing and copy cannot supply that missing relevance.

### Improve credibility through a demonstrable path

Ask what the buyer can inspect before purchasing and what they can verify during delivery. A sample using clearly labeled fictional data demonstrates a format. A real product demonstration supports a capability. An authorized case study may support a specific customer outcome, within its actual circumstances. Keep these evidence types distinct.

When proof is weak, narrow the promise and expose the method. Show what the customer supplies, what you do and what counts as done. A seller's confidence is not evidence that the buyer will achieve a business result. Avoid disguising an early experiment as a proven system.

### Reduce delay without moving work out of sight

Separate time to first useful output from time to the final desired outcome. A customer could leave a setup call with a working list today, while seeing whether it reduces repeated checking takes longer. State both if they matter. “Ready in one session” must not quietly mean “after three days of customer preparation.”

List prerequisites next to the timeline. If the customer has to collect current lesson changes before a call, the delivery promise begins only once the necessary inputs are available. Explain missing-input handling so the seller does not rush an incomplete handoff to satisfy a headline.

### Reduce effort without creating unlimited service

Assisted setup can lower the customer's burden while increasing yours. Identify the part you can remove: formatting a first list, explaining the status field, or demonstrating one update. Do not promise to handle all future administration unless you have priced and staffed that service.

Choose deliverables by obstacle. A short guide to updating status answers a usage obstacle. A library of unrelated marketing templates does not necessarily improve a confirmation-tracking offer. Every extra element should have a reason, an owner and a limit; otherwise a bundle becomes harder to deliver and harder to understand.

## Compare offer versions before selecting one

Create a few meaningfully different versions: self-service, assisted setup, or a managed service, for example. Compare customer effort, evidence, delivery time, support exposure and proposed price. The most generous version may be the least feasible. A narrower version can still fail if the customer does not value the problem enough.

Use a rough delivery model before writing buyer-facing copy. Sum preparation, live work, handoff, follow-up and an explicit support allowance. Calculate the number of customers you can serve within available hours, leaving the stated buffer intact. Do not divide capacity only by the live call and forget the rest of the job.

Keep the economic quantities named. Price minus direct cash cost is a cash contribution before labor and overhead. Subtracting labor valued at an assumed rate produces a different estimate. Neither is net profit unless all relevant costs are included. A positive estimate establishes arithmetic under assumptions, not market demand.

Test a plausible overrun. If one extra support hour wipes out the estimate or a few customers exhaust the week, change scope, delivery design, capacity or price before opening more slots. Increasing price may improve arithmetic but still require a separate willingness-to-pay test.

## Commitments the delivery team can honor

An offer includes more than the headline. Specify deliverables, scope limits, dependencies, timing, price, payment assumptions and exclusions. If you propose a remedy for missed delivery, identify the triggering event, evidence and responsible person. Leave unresolved commercial terms visibly unresolved until the business decides them.

A business-controlled milestone is easier to promise than an external result. Completing a configured list is different from guaranteeing every student replies. Removing the impossible promise should lead to a clearer offer, not a vague disclaimer below an unchanged headline.

Use capacity truthfully. “Two slots this week” requires actual available slots and a process that updates when they fill. It is not a permanent decorative line. If there is no real deadline, describe the normal booking process instead. A precise promise with no countdown can still be useful.

## Evaluate objections before changing the package

An objection about effort may justify assisted setup. An objection about manual maintenance may reveal a product limitation that a call cannot solve. An objection about price needs context: compared with what alternative and for which result? Record the objection and the part of the offer it challenges before adding extras or discounting.

| Finding | Likely issue to investigate | Better next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buyers expect an outcome you cannot control | Promise exceeds delivery | Rewrite the outcome and acceptance point |
| Setup is easy but ongoing work is disliked | Recurring product burden | Test product fit, not more onboarding bonuses |
| Every sale needs custom extras | Scope mismatch | Define variants or a narrower target buyer |
| Support overruns the estimate | Hidden delivery cost | Measure support and adjust scope or capacity |
| Customers understand but choose the free alternative | Value or buyer fit | Investigate the switching reason |
| The package looks profitable only without founder time | Incomplete accounting | Show both cash and labor-adjusted estimates |

A useful first test checks both sides: does a suitable buyer choose the defined offer, and can you fulfill it within the stated scope? Save the actual delivery time and exceptions if a pilot runs. Do not treat a polite reaction to the offer as a sale, or a sale as proof that delivery is sustainable.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can the value equation determine my price?** No. It structures a discussion about perceived value. Pricing also depends on evidence about alternatives, demand, costs, and the buying context.

**Do I need a guarantee?** No. Only propose commitments you can explain and fulfill. Clear scope and honest proof may be more appropriate for an early product.

**Does a stronger offer guarantee sales?** No. The right buyers still need to encounter it, understand it, and choose it.
