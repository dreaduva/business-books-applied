A buyer hears “tutoring management platform” and asks about invoices, student records, and payroll. The founder wants to demonstrate a small tool for tracking lesson confirmations. Both people can understand the words and still be having different conversations.

This guide helps you decide what customers should compare your product with, why its differences matter, and which description creates accurate expectations. Its output is a positioning brief with reasoning you can inspect, rather than a sentence that sounds convincing only inside the company.

## Summary

April Dunford treats positioning as the context that makes a product's differentiated value understandable. Her public introduction connects five components: competitive alternatives, differentiated capabilities, customer value, best-fit customers, and market category. The components depend on one another: a difference matters relative to an alternative, its value depends on the customer, and a category should help that customer understand the value. Positioning is distinct from a tagline, messaging, or a company vision. [Author's positioning introduction](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning).

The practical implication is to expose your assumptions before compressing them into copy. If you cannot explain which alternative a capability improves on, writing a stronger adjective will not supply the missing comparison. If you know the comparison but cannot show why the buyer cares, adding a larger market label will not establish relevance.

## Who should use this toolkit?

Use it when suitable buyers misunderstand your product, comparisons keep pulling the conversation toward things you do not provide, or your team disagrees about the reason customers choose you. It also helps a new product make its initial positioning assumptions explicit.

A product without customer evidence gets a **provisional positioning thesis** here. It can be coherent and worth testing without being established. An existing product can use actual buying alternatives, observed usage, and win/loss notes to challenge its brief. Neither situation calls for invented customer certainty.

Choose the scope before beginning. Positioning one feature, one product, and a company with several products can require different comparisons. This worksheet focuses on one offer or product at a time. If your team mixes them, first identify which buying decision the brief is meant to support.

## Prepare the evidence before the workshop

Bring material that can settle a disagreement: a product demonstration, notes from recent buying decisions, reasons customers retained their old process, and examples of how the product is used. Include unsuccessful or unsuitable cases. If you only examine enthusiastic customers, your definition of fit can become circular.

Our worksheet assigns evidence IDs so a reviewer can trace a claim. It distinguishes product facts, customer reports, observations, and assumptions. A customer saying they save time is a report; a measured before-and-after task comparison is a different kind of evidence. Record either honestly without converting it into the other.

| Input | What it helps establish | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product demonstration | A capability exists under shown conditions | Its economic value to every buyer |
| Recent purchase account | Alternatives considered in that decision | Every alternative in the market |
| Usage observation | How a particular person uses the product | Why all customers chose it |
| Lost opportunity | A specific objection or preference | That every similar buyer will reject it |
| Founder hypothesis | A candidate explanation to investigate | A customer fact |

Keep the first brief small enough that the team can disagree about its actual reasoning. An inventory of fifty features is not automatically a stronger starting point than a few capabilities tied to a meaningful comparison.

## Start with the buyer's actual alternatives

Dunford distinguishes the status quo from the products a buyer seriously considers when searching. Existing routines can be cheap and adequate; a company that resembles yours may never enter that buyer's shortlist. Treating all lookalike products as equally relevant can weaken the comparison. [Positioning and Competition](https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/positioning-and-competition).

In our fictional tutor case, one person maintains a calendar and checks message threads. Another has an existing platform with a confirmation view. Their alternatives differ even though both are tutors. Comparing only against scheduling startups would miss the first person's routine; comparing only against messages would ignore the second person's adequate solution.

Ask about an actual decision: what did the person use, consider, or retain? Record doing nothing as a concrete continuation of the current routine. “No competitor” often conceals a workaround or a problem that the customer does not consider worth solving.

Keep a separate watchlist for possible future competitors. It can inform product strategy without automatically determining today's positioning. If a previously hypothetical rival starts appearing in real decisions, update the comparison with that evidence.

### A useful alternative record

Write the alternative, who uses or considers it, why it is retained, and the evidence ID. “Spreadsheet” alone is incomplete. A buyer may prefer it because their team already knows it, because it handles exceptions, or because switching would require moving years of records. Those reasons affect what your product must make worthwhile.

Do not assume that inconvenience creates switching intent. A customer can acknowledge a problem and still rationally prefer the existing routine. The brief should explain the relevant advantage and the work required to obtain it.

## Connect capabilities to a reason to switch

A feature comparison answers what differs. A value explanation answers what that difference enables for this buyer. Our worksheet keeps the chain visible:

**Alternative → demonstrated difference → useful consequence → evidence and limit.**

For a confirmation tracker, “a pending list” is a capability. “A tutor can review unresolved changes together” describes a direct consequence. “Fewer missed lessons” is a further outcome that depends on more than displaying a list. Evidence for the capability does not automatically support that outcome.

| Weak claim | More useful statement | What still needs checking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| We are easier | A tutor can filter all pending changes in one view | Whether that is easier than their actual routine |
| We save hours | The product removes the need to open each thread just to locate the recorded status | Whether users keep the status current and how long their old task took |
| Our AI is unique | Identify the specific working behavior and relevant alternative | Whether the difference matters to the buying decision |
| We prevent no-shows | Describe the confirmation capability accurately | Attendance outcomes require separate evidence |

Do not force every feature to become a differentiator. Sign-in, export, or ordinary security features may be important requirements without being the reason to choose the product. A feature shared with an alternative can still matter to delivery; it simply cannot support an exclusive advantage on its own.

Dunford's discussion of differentiation warns that teams can miss the customer's view of a difference. Investigate why customers choose a product before concluding that it has no meaningful distinction. That investigation can also expose an unsupported claim rather than rescue it. [The No Differentiation Illusion](https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/the-no-differentiation-illusion).

### Handle tradeoffs, not just benefits

If your product consolidates information but requires manual updates, include both facts. The benefit may matter to someone with many unresolved changes and be unnecessary work for someone with a stable schedule. A credible value argument can be narrower than the founder originally hoped.

Group related capabilities around a consequence when that improves understanding. Avoid producing one vague benefit for each feature merely to complete a table. Keep an outcome unquantified if you have no reliable basis for a number.

## Define best-fit customers through the situation

A useful segment explains why the differentiated value matters. “Small businesses” is rarely enough for this task. Even “independent tutors” leaves open whether lessons change, who confirms them, what system is already in place, and whether maintaining another tool is acceptable.

In the example, the initial candidate is a tutor who regularly coordinates changes manually and needs an overview of unresolved items. That is a hypothesis about a situation, not a claim that all tutors share it. Explicit poor-fit cases make the choice easier to challenge: a tutor with a satisfactory confirmation system, a center requiring payroll, or someone whose schedule almost never changes.

Record the user and buyer separately when they differ. A tutor may use the tool while an administrator approves software. A brief aimed at the user should not quietly presume purchasing authority. Similarly, a customer's enthusiasm does not reveal the organization's budget or approval path.

| Candidate segment | Why the value might matter | Reason to hesitate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solo tutor with frequent manual changes | An unresolved-item overview may address an existing task | Manual status entry may add work |
| Tutor using an adequate booking platform | Familiar with confirmation tracking | The same value may already be supplied |
| Multi-tutor center | More coordination may create a larger need | Permissions, records, and procurement may exceed the product |

Avoid choosing the largest-sounding segment as a substitute for evidence of fit. Also avoid turning a tiny sample into a permanent exclusion rule. Record why you are focusing now and what new evidence would justify revisiting the boundary.

## Choose a category by the expectations it creates

Dunford describes market category as context that points buyers toward the product's value. It creates assumptions about capabilities, alternatives, and the buying situation. It cannot replace the rest of the positioning or carry the entire sales explanation. [Understanding the Job of a Market Category](https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/understanding-the-job-of-a-market).

Our category exercise compares the expectations a label creates with the product you can actually demonstrate. It does not award points for sounding larger or more novel.

| Candidate description | Likely expectation to investigate | Product implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tutoring management platform | Billing, records, and broader operations | Misleading if the product only tracks confirmations |
| Scheduling software | Availability and booking | Requires explanation if booking is absent |
| Lesson-confirmation tracker | Tracking the state of changed lessons | Closer to scope, but automatic messaging may still be assumed |

These are hypotheses about audience interpretation. Ask relevant readers what they expect rather than treating the team's guess as an observed fact.

A plain descriptive phrase can be useful without being an established market category. Label it accordingly. Do not claim that a category is recognized, growing, or led by your product without evidence. Creating a new label also creates an explanation burden: people must understand what it refers to before they can judge the offer.

When two candidates remain plausible, compare them on the buying context you actually need. Which one helps people identify a relevant use and makes fewer incorrect assumptions? Preserve important disagreements. You can choose provisionally and name the evidence that would change the choice.

## Assemble the brief before writing the headline

The brief should include the intended buyer situation, actual alternatives, differentiated capabilities, supported value, poor-fit conditions, category or descriptive context, and the remaining assumptions. A short paragraph can summarize it, but should not replace the evidence beneath it.

A practical review is to trace the argument backward. If the proposed benefit disappeared, which capability would be missing? If a buyer already had that capability in their existing tool, why would they switch? If the answer changes by segment, split the cases rather than writing one sentence broad enough to hide the conflict.

Only then translate the brief into [landing-page messaging](../building-a-storybrand/README.md). Public copy should use the supported claim level. Do not turn a provisional internal hypothesis into “proven,” “leading,” or a fabricated percentage when moving it to the homepage.

## Worked example: the broader label creates the wrong conversation

The [completed example](worked-example.md) starts with a fictional founder calling LessonLoop an AI-powered management platform. The supplied product facts support neither AI automation nor broad management capabilities. Buyer notes include a manual-message user, a satisfied platform user, a low-frequency user, and a center with requirements beyond the product.

The example traces those inputs into a candidate brief, compares three descriptions, and shows a comprehension exercise that exposes another problem: a reader assumes confirmations are collected automatically. The revision makes manual status recording explicit. Better comprehension still does not establish that the buyer will pay or adopt the extra routine.

This is why a worked example needs the intermediate decisions. A finished paragraph alone can conceal unsupported claims, unsuitable buyers, and unresolved disadvantages.

## Test the brief without leading the answer

For a comprehension check, present the same limited description and ask what the person thinks the product does, who it seems intended for, and what work they expect to do themselves. Capture their explanation before correcting it. Asking “Is it clear that this tracks confirmations manually?” supplies the answer you need to investigate.

For relevance, ask about the current workflow and what, if anything, the described capability changes. For commercial evidence, examine actual decisions about a defined offer. These are different questions. Someone correctly describing the product is evidence of understanding, not willingness to pay.

Record recruitment and which version each person saw. If you revise the description halfway through, keep those responses separate. Do not combine them into a single “validated positioning” score. Small qualitative checks can expose misunderstandings without estimating their prevalence.

## Troubleshoot the positioning

**Every competitor appears on the list.** Separate actual alternatives from the market watchlist. Ask which appeared in recent decisions. Retain unknowns where customer evidence is absent.

**The only difference is “better service.”** Identify what is delivered differently: a specific response commitment, implementation assistance, or expertise. Then establish whether it matters and whether you can support the comparison.

**Two customer groups want different things.** Draft their capability-to-value chains separately. Choose a focus using evidence and operating constraints, or maintain separate briefs if the business genuinely serves both. Averaging them often erases the useful distinction.

**The category sounds right but creates irrelevant questions.** Inspect which expectations the label triggers. Try a narrower description and clarify essential exclusions. Do not add nonexistent features to make the category fit.

**Readers understand but do not care.** A clarity improvement does not repair weak value. Revisit the consequence, the current alternative, the segment, and the burden of switching.

**A new feature changes the story.** Update the product facts first. Decide whether it changes the differentiated value or merely adds convenience. Revisit affected claims instead of renaming the whole business automatically.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is this the book's official positioning worksheet?** No. It is an original evidence-and-decision worksheet applying the author's publicly explained ideas.

**Can a prelaunch product use it?** Yes, as a provisional thesis. Product facts can be demonstrated before customers exist; customer alternatives and value may remain hypotheses.

**Does differentiation mean no other product can do it?** This brief compares relevant alternatives under specified conditions. Avoid universal uniqueness claims when you only know a narrow comparison.

**Must the final output be one sentence?** No. A concise summary helps communication, but reviewers need the reasoning and evidence that support it.

**Which edition is pictured?** The original 2019 edition, ISBN 9781999023003. The author now offers an expanded edition. This guide does not claim to reproduce either edition's full process or chapter structure.
