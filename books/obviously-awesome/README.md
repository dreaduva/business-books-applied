A product can work and still be difficult to place. Before rewriting its homepage, work out what a buyer would compare it with. This toolkit produces a **positioning brief**: a defensible connection between a customer's present alternative and a reason to switch.

## Summary

April Dunford treats positioning as the context that makes a product's value understandable. Her public introduction connects five components: competitive alternatives, distinctive capabilities, customer value, customers who particularly need that value, and market category. Start with what buyers would actually do without your product, then work through those dependencies. A category is useful when it creates accurate expectations. A slogan cannot resolve missing evidence about the buyer or the product.

## Apply the positioning framework

Our worksheet adds an evidence trail to each decision. Record the source of an alternative, the product behavior behind a capability, and the buyer situation that makes the resulting benefit matter. “Simple,” “innovative,” and “AI-powered” are insufficient on their own. They do not explain what changes in a customer's working day.

Consider a lesson-confirmation tool. A tutor may currently use a spreadsheet and individual messages. The relevant comparison might be that routine, even if the founder has spent weeks studying scheduling software. If the product merely sends messages faster, show how; don't claim it eliminates missed lessons without evidence.

| Decision | Useful evidence | Weak substitute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Present alternative | A recent account of the existing routine | A list of every company in the market |
| Differentiated capability | A demonstrable behavior absent from that routine | An adjective |
| Customer value | A consequence the buyer cares about | A feature renamed as a benefit |
| Best-fit customer | A situation that increases the need | “All small businesses” |
| Category | Expectations the product can meet | A larger-sounding market label |

## When the positioning is still a hypothesis

A new product may have no customers. You can draft candidate positioning, but label the alternatives and value claims as assumptions. The next step is to investigate them, not announce that a target market is proven. Revisit the brief when the product, alternatives, or buyer situation changes.

The worksheet asks you to compare two category candidates and write down the expectations each creates. Reject an attractive label if it suggests capabilities you cannot supply. This is particularly useful for AI products: a buyer hearing “assistant” may expect much more autonomy than a narrow drafting tool provides.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a positioning statement the finished output?** A short statement can communicate a decision. Keep the evidence and reasoning beside it so another person can challenge the decision.

**Can this improve landing-page copy?** Yes, by giving the writer a specific buyer, comparison, and supportable benefit. It does not establish conversion performance.

**Which edition does this cover?** The cover is the original 2019 edition. The author now offers an expanded edition; this application uses her public positioning introduction and does not claim to reproduce either edition's full process.

**What should I do before this?** Use [The Mom Test](../the-mom-test/README.md) to investigate the existing routine, or [The Lean Startup](../the-lean-startup/README.md) to test an uncertain product assumption.
