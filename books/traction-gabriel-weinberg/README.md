Choosing a channel because you enjoy using it is an expensive way to learn. This toolkit helps you compare routes to a specific customer, run small tests, and choose what deserves more attention.

**This is Traction by Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, the customer-acquisition book.** It is different from Gino Wickman's book about running a business.

## Summary

Weinberg and Mares describe nineteen acquisition channels and a selection approach called Bullseye. The central idea is to consider a broad set of possibilities, investigate promising routes, and concentrate effort where evidence supports it. Product development alone does not create a reliable path to customers. The appropriate route depends on the business and its stage; a channel's popularity does not establish its usefulness for your offer.

## A Bullseye worksheet you can run

Our original worksheet turns that broad-to-focused approach into a test record. First define a meaningful acquisition event. For a paid product, a signup may only be an intermediate event. For a free tool, an activated user may be appropriate. Keep that definition stable while comparing tests.

Next, write a reason each candidate could reach the intended audience. Include routes outside your usual habits. Then choose a small shortlist you can actually evaluate within your cash and time limits. The worksheet does not prescribe a universal number of tests or a universal success threshold.

| Candidate | Why it might fit | Small test | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Specialist newsletter | Readers share the relevant job | One approved placement | One issue may not represent the channel |
| Search content | People already seek help with the task | One useful answer page | Organic discovery takes time |
| Partner demonstration | A trusted provider reaches likely users | One agreed session | Partner access may be hard to repeat |

## Compare outcomes without fooling yourself

Record cash, labor, exposure, qualified responses, activation, and paid customers separately. “Cost per signup” is not customer acquisition cost. A cash-free channel can still require substantial founder time. If you estimate a value for that time, show it as an assumption and preserve the raw hours.

Small tests are especially vulnerable to attribution errors. A buyer who saw a newsletter and later searched your brand should not quietly count as two customers. State the attribution rule and what you cannot track. Do not infer that the lowest observed cost will remain constant when spending increases.

## Deciding what to try next

A useful test can reveal that a channel reaches the wrong people, that the offer is unclear, or that the measurement is broken. Those require different responses. If no one saw the offer, you learned little about demand. If qualified visitors arrived but did not understand it, revisit the message before blaming the channel.

Choose a next test with a specific reason, budget ceiling, and review point. Focusing means making a deliberate allocation, not declaring a permanent winner.

## The channel landscape, before the shortlist

Weinberg's [nineteen-channel overview](https://medium.com/@yegg/the-19-channels-you-can-use-to-get-traction-93c762d19339) names targeting blogs, publicity, unconventional PR, search engine marketing, social/display ads, offline ads, SEO, content marketing, email marketing, viral marketing, engineering as marketing, business development, sales, affiliate programs, existing platforms, trade shows, offline events, speaking engagements and community building. These are routes to consider, not a checklist of campaigns to launch.

For a first pass, write one plausible audience encounter for each relevant route. Who would discover you, in what context, and why might they take the next step? A channel name such as “partnerships” is not yet a strategy. A named type of tutor association, an agreed demonstration and a destination for interested tutors is more concrete.

Do not discard a route simply because you dislike using it. Equally, do not put a distant possibility into this month's shortlist without the access, budget or skills to test it. Keep a deferred list with the missing prerequisite. That preserves ideas without confusing them with work you can execute now.

## What Bullseye changes about the decision

In his [Bullseye explanation](https://medium.com/@yegg/the-bullseye-framework-for-getting-traction-ef49d05bfd7e), Weinberg describes moving from possibilities to inexpensive tests to concentration on a promising route. The tests examine acquisition cost, available customers and customer suitability; focusing follows evidence rather than personal preference. The practical methods below are our original way of recording those decisions.

A useful shortlist contains different reasons a route could work. Three nearly identical advertisements are creative variants inside a channel, not three independent distribution hypotheses. At the same time, a comparison of unrelated offers tells you little about the route itself. Hold the audience definition and intended outcome stable where possible, and document unavoidable differences.

Start with the next business question. If the team needs five suitable pilot users, raw readership is a weak finish line. If the business has a functioning paid offer, activated free users may still be an intermediate stage. Select a meaningful event that the test can observe and explicitly name what it cannot establish yet.

### Design a test that can fail informatively

Write the audience, offer, access method, budget, observation window and outcome before running it. Add a failure interpretation: what would zero results mean? If no one saw a partner invitation because the partner never sent it, the test failed to obtain distribution; it did not demonstrate that customers dislike the product.

Set a cash ceiling and a labor ceiling. The founder may have enough money to buy three placements but insufficient time to follow up with all respondents. A test that overloads follow-up can make a good source appear to convert poorly. Keep response ownership and capacity in the plan.

Distinguish the exposure period from follow-up. A one-day event can produce conversations that mature over several weeks. Save when each prospect first arrived and when they became eligible for the outcome you measure. Do not compare a two-week-old cohort with one that joined yesterday as though both had the same chance to activate.

### Preserve a reconstructable funnel

For each route, record the count at each meaningful stage and its definition. “Qualified” might mean an independent tutor handling at least three lesson changes in the last month, if that is your chosen target. “Activated” might mean adding a real change and returning to check its status. These are project decisions, not universal definitions from the book.

Store enough event-level detail to check totals without unnecessarily collecting personal data: anonymous identifier, channel, first contact date, qualification result, activation date and follow-up outcome. Record unknown rather than false when a result has not been observed. If two routes reach the same person, use a disclosed attribution rule and keep the overlap visible.

Cash per lead is cash divided by leads. Cash per activated user uses activated users. Customer acquisition cost should use actual customers under a stated accounting scope. Zero observed customers does not produce a zero cost per customer. Report the spend and zero count; a finite observed per-customer cost cannot be calculated.

Labor can reverse the apparent ranking. Show hours first, then an optional valuation using a stated hourly assumption. Never mix cash-only figures for one route with cash-plus-labor figures for another. Keep costs already incurred separate from the future cost of repeating the test.

### Decide what the numbers do and do not justify

A cheap source can attract people who only want the free resource. A costly source can attract excellent customers but exhaust the founder's time. A promising partner may be a one-off favor. Look at suitability, follow-through and repeatable access alongside cost.

A small sample can support a bounded repeat without proving a stable rate. Record the alternative explanation most likely to overturn your choice. Perhaps the partner audience was unusually engaged, or the newsletter placement used a weak headline. Choose the next test to examine that explanation instead of immediately multiplying spend.

If every route brings appropriate people who abandon the same step, inspect the common offer or onboarding. If each route fails in a different way, retain those differences. “Marketing didn't work” hides the information needed to choose another experiment.

## A practical decision table

| Observed situation | What it supports | Next move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No verified distribution | Access or measurement failure | Verify delivery before judging demand |
| Many visitors, few suitable prospects | Possible audience mismatch | Inspect source context and qualification |
| Suitable prospects misunderstand the offer | Messaging problem is plausible | Repair the same promise before changing route |
| Activation is good but repeat use is weak | Acquisition alone is insufficient | Inspect ongoing value or onboarding |
| Good downstream behavior, high labor | Capacity constraint | Test a repeatable delivery method and cost it |
| One channel has early results; another is still maturing | Unequal evidence | Compare only eligible cohorts or delay the decision |

Record the selected allocation, what is paused, the next review point and the condition that would reverse the choice. “Focus” should result in a calendar and budget someone can follow. It need not become a permanent belief about the channel.

## Moving from selection to execution

Once a route earns a bounded repeat, use [$100M Leads](../100m-leads/README.md) to develop the campaign, qualification and follow-up. Return here when deciding whether the route deserves more resources or whether another channel should be tested. This separation keeps campaign improvements from being mistaken for evidence that every channel has been compared.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does this cover all nineteen channels?** The guide names the full set for brainstorming; the toolkit goes deep on comparing bounded tests rather than reproducing nineteen book chapters.

**Can I compare SEO with a one-day advertisement?** Not on immediate results alone. Their time horizons differ; record that limitation and use appropriate observation windows.

**Do clicks prove traction?** They show an early interaction. Define the behavior that matters for your business and follow the funnel to that point.

Before testing acquisition, use [Building a StoryBrand](../building-a-storybrand/README.md) to make the offer and next action understandable.
