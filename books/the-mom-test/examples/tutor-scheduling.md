# Worked Example: Tutor Scheduling

**Fictional teaching example.** All participant details, conversations, and observations on this page are invented for illustration. They are not research findings.

## Starting situation

A founder is considering a calendar product for independent tutors. The founder suspects that lesson changes create repeated administrative work but has not established whether the problem is frequent, expensive, or poorly served by existing tools.

## Completed interview plan

**Decision to inform:** whether to investigate lesson-change coordination before designing a new calendar.

**Learning objective:** understand a recent schedule change from request to confirmation, including the people and tools involved.

**Relevant participant:** an independent tutor who has recently rearranged an individual lesson and normally communicates directly with students or parents.

**Recruitment limit:** personal contacts may use similar tools or work in similar markets. Their experience may not represent tutors generally.

**Assumption to challenge:** current calendars are the main source of the difficulty.

**Evidence that would change the founder's view:** participants already handle changes easily, changes rarely occur, or the real difficulty lies outside scheduling.

## Conversation guide

1. Briefly explain that you are learning how tutors handle lesson changes; do not describe a planned app.
2. Ask the tutor to describe the last lesson they rescheduled.
3. Follow the sequence: who requested the change, what happened next, and when it was considered confirmed.
4. Ask which tools and people were involved.
5. Explore the consequences without suggesting that the situation was necessarily painful.
6. Ask what they have tried differently and why they kept or abandoned it.
7. Close by checking what you misunderstood and whether a follow-up would be appropriate.

## Decision after the example

Do not build a replacement calendar from this note. Investigate whether the confirmation issue appears in other relevant conversations and whether existing tools already solve it. If a meaningful pattern appears, formulate a specific offer and an appropriate test of behavior.

The point is the change in the question: from “Would tutors like a nicer calendar?” to “What happens when several people must agree on a changed lesson?”
