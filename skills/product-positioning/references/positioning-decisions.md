# Positioning Decisions

An original procedure for constructing a traceable brief. Use the user's evidence rather than completing fields with plausible invented facts.

## Scope and readiness

Identify the product or offer, current capabilities, intended buying decision, and any distinction between user and buyer. Position today's deliverable. Keep roadmap capabilities separate. For a prelaunch product, demonstrate product facts where possible and label the customer/value argument a provisional thesis.

Ask for missing information only when it materially changes the decision. With sparse input, a candidate brief and a few explicit unknowns can be useful. Do not manufacture interviews to make the brief look complete.

## Alternatives

Record the existing routine and actual shortlist options with their source and situation. An alternative may be adequate; state why it is retained. Do not assume inconvenience proves willingness to switch. Separate a competitive watchlist from alternatives supported by buying evidence.

If two segments use different alternatives, build separate comparisons before choosing a focus. A capability may differentiate against manual work while offering nothing additional against a well-equipped incumbent. Avoid declaring universal uniqueness from the narrower comparison.

## Capability, consequence, outcome

Use this chain: demonstrated capability → difference from named alternative → useful consequence for the specified situation → evidence and limits.

A pending filter supports a consolidated view. A consolidated view may make review easier. Reduced missed appointments requires further evidence. Keep these levels distinct, especially when the user requests numerical claims. A customer's report can support an attributed account; it is not automatically an independently measured result.

Retain important requirements that do not differentiate. Remove generic adjectives unless they can be translated into a specific behavior and comparison. Include disadvantages and the work needed to obtain the benefit. Do not present a feature's absence as a virtue without explaining why the target buyer would prefer that tradeoff.

## Fit boundary

Explain which circumstances make the difference valuable: workflow, existing tools, frequency, constraints, or decision roles. Give poor-fit situations with reasons. Do not make a universal exclusion rule from a single lost opportunity.

When selecting one segment, state the evidence and operating constraints behind the choice. A larger addressable market is not evidence of better product fit. If no segment can be supported yet, return competing hypotheses and the evidence needed to distinguish them.

## Category comparison

Compare candidates only where the decision is material. For each, record expected capabilities, likely alternatives and buyer context, product fit, and potentially misleading implications. Distinguish team expectations from observed audience interpretations.

A plain description can be a sensible provisional choice without constituting a recognized category. Do not invent category leadership or market size. If the user insists on a broad label, explain the expectations it creates and propose a qualifier, while retaining any unresolved mismatch.

Choose based on helping the relevant buyer understand the supportable value. Avoid arbitrary weighted scores that imply precision the evidence cannot sustain. Where two categories remain plausible, recommend a specific comprehension question or buying-context observation that could separate them.

## Brief

Produce the concise argument and retain its evidence beneath it. Include buyer situation, actual alternative, capability, consequence, context, and limitations as relevant. A tagline can follow, but it is not a substitute for the decisions. Do not turn internal assumptions into factual public promises when summarizing.
