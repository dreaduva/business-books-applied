# Questions That Produce Usable Evidence

These are original practice patterns, not quotations or an official worksheet.

## Start from a decision

“I want to build scheduling software” is an idea. “Should we investigate lesson-change coordination?” is a decision conversations can inform. List what could make the idea unnecessary: rare incidents, an adequate workaround, a different buyer, or a more important problem.

Choose participants by their relevant experience. Asking only people who complain can help understand difficult incidents, but cannot tell you how common those incidents are. Include contrasting experiences when frequency is the question.

## Repair the mechanism, not just the wording

| Problem | Weak prompt | Better prompt | Follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pitch disguised as research | Would our reminders save you time? | Walk me through the last lesson change. | How did everyone learn the new time? |
| Assumed pain | Why is your calendar frustrating? | What worked and what did not in that instance? | What happened as a result? |
| Vague frequency | Do changes happen constantly? | Which changes can you recall from the last teaching week? | How does that compare with other weeks? |
| Hypothetical price | Would you pay $20? | What do you currently spend on handling this work? | Who chose that arrangement? |
| Feature request | Should we add a group chat? | Tell me about the situation that made you ask for that. | What have you tried instead? |
| Leading comparison | Was texting worse than an app? | How did you choose the approach you used? | What alternatives did you consider? |

An inability to recall an incident is missing evidence, not proof that the problem never happens. Not searching for software can have several explanations; ask about constraints rather than treating it as an automatic rejection.

## Recover a drifting conversation

- Compliment: acknowledge it briefly, then return to what the participant currently does.
- General claim: ask for a specific occasion and the steps involved.
- Feature idea: investigate the situation behind it before discussing implementation.
- Unclear magnitude: ask how the person knows, or whether a record exists; do not supply a number.
- Contradiction: describe the apparent difference neutrally and ask what changed between the situations.

Listen for constraints and tradeoffs, not only dramatic complaints. Do not turn every response into another scripted question; pursue details that bear on the decision.

## Invitation and opening

Invitation pattern—adapt only truthful details:

> I'm researching how [relevant group] handles [situation]. Would you be open to a short conversation about a recent example? I'm trying to understand the current process, rather than sell a product. It is fine if your current setup works well. If you're interested, what time would suit you?

Opening:

> Thanks for talking with me. I'd like to understand how this works today. There are no right answers, and you can skip anything. Is it okay if I take notes?

Do not conceal an existing commercial purpose. If recording is useful, ask explicitly before recording and agree how the material will be used. Do not put customer identifiers or confidential records into a public repository.

## Close with an appropriate request

First summarize what you understood and invite corrections. If another person handles the decision, ask whether an introduction would be appropriate. If an artifact would resolve uncertainty, ask whether a redacted example can be shared. Make declining easy; record whether the action actually occurs.
