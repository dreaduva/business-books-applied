# From Experiment Results to a Next Decision

Use this procedure for supplied results. Preserve the original plan as a historical record. A retrospective explanation should never masquerade as a predeclared rule.

## Reconstruct what was actually observed

Identify exposure, eligibility, the unit of observation, intervention version, window, and source. Check whether multiple rows are events from one participant or independent participants. Retain identifiers so findings can be traced back to records.

Classify complete outcomes, pending outcomes, missing data, and exclusions separately. Exclusion reasons should follow the original rules where available. If a rule is unclear, show the ambiguity and its effect rather than quietly selecting the favorable interpretation.

A contact list is not exposure. A signup is not use. Use is not a purchase. A purchase is not retention. Report the specific observed behavior.

## Calculate with the original denominator

Show the raw fraction before a percentage. With zero eligible observations, the rate is undefined; it is not zero success or complete failure. If there are five known successes, two known failures, and three unknown outcomes among ten eligible events, a complete-case rate is 5/7, but the full-data success rate could range from 5/10 to 8/10. Explain why missingness matters; do not select 5/7 without qualification.

For effort or cost, check units and include relevant preparation or exception work. Where a median masks expensive exceptions, show the total, range, or exception burden as appropriate. Do not compute per-customer economics from per-event effort without a justified link between events and customers.

Cohort comparisons require matching definitions and maturity windows. Differences can reflect audience, timing, or intervention changes. Describe those limitations before attributing a result to the product.

## Review the rule and the mechanism

State pass, fail, or indeterminate for each original criterion. Then ask why. Keep these explanations distinct:

- Recruitment/exposure: the relevant people did not encounter the intended opportunity.
- Execution: participants encountered a broken or different intervention.
- Measurement: the observation cannot reliably answer the question.
- Hypothesis: adequate execution produced behavior inconsistent with the assumption.

Several can be present together. Failed execution is itself a real result about the current delivery path, even when it limits what can be inferred about demand.

## Choose among meaningful alternatives

Compare the live options: repair the test, continue with a bounded investment, change a substantive assumption, stop, or obtain a specific missing fact. Explain what evidence supports the preferred option and what contrary evidence weakens it.

Do not recommend a pivot solely because a small test disappointed, or perseverance solely because someone expressed interest. A strategic change must say which customer, problem, offer, channel, or delivery assumption changes. A cosmetic change is not a strategic pivot by virtue of its label.

## Revise prospectively

Record the new hypothesis, intervention change, measurement definition, cap, and decision rule. List what stays fixed and what makes the next result hard to compare. If an old rule was poorly chosen, explain the revision and keep the old result assessed under the old rule.

Finish with a concise decision statement: what to do, why, what remains unknown, and which observation could overturn the recommendation. Never fill a result field with fictional numbers unless the user explicitly requested an illustrative scenario and those numbers are labeled as invented.
